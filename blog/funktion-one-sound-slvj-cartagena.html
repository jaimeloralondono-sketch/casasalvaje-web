The body listens too: the Funktion-One sound of SLVJ's club in Cartagena

From the picó sound systems of the barrios to the British speakers at SLVJ, the story of a coast that has always measured the night in watts.

Leer en español →

Past midnight, the bass isn't heard: it's felt. It rises through the stone floor, settles in your chest and suddenly nobody in the room is standing still. A club can have the best music and the best crowd, but if the sound fails, the night falls flat. That's why, when the SLVJ room turns into a club, it plays on Funktion-One.

I · The inheritance

A coast measured in speakers

On Colombia's Caribbean coast, sound is close to a religion. Since the street parties of the mid-20th century, the neighbourhoods of Cartagena and Barranquilla have gathered around picós, huge sound systems whose name comes from the English "pick-up". Their owners competed for the rarest records, many of them arriving from Africa through the port, and for the glory of bass you could feel in your chest blocks away.

Out of that culture, around the early 1980s, came champeta: music sung in Cartagena, with roots in San Basilio de Palenque, the town founded by escaped slaves led by Benkos Biohó and recognised by UNESCO in 2005 as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. Its first dances were called "therapy".

In Cartagena, a sound system is never a technical detail. It's an inheritance.

II · The machine

What is Funktion-One

Funktion-One is a British sound system maker that became a benchmark in electronic music and in the world's most demanding clubs. Its promise is clarity: bass you feel without being battered, and voices and instruments that stay distinct even at high volume.

It was founded in 1992 by Tony Andrews and John Newsham, the same engineers who had earlier created Turbosound, and the "k" in the name is a nod to 1970s funk. In its own way it's a British picó: obsessed with bass, with clarity and with making sure nobody stands still.

III · The night

How it feels at SLVJ

Thursday to Saturday, after dinner, the feast's lights go down and the room lights up. Stone, velvet, amber light and a sound built for dancing until 3 am without leaving with ringing ears.

There's something of magical realism in the change. The same room where dinner was served becomes something else without anyone leaving their seat, as if the house had two lives and chose, at a certain hour, to reveal the second.
