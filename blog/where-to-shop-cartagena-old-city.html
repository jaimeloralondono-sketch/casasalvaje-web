Where to shop in Cartagena: Colombian design in the old city

From St Dom to Johanna Ortiz: the shops that dress the walled city.

Leer en español →

The old city isn't just for walking and eating: it's also the best place in Colombia to shop for design. These are the shops that live up to the walled city, all within walking distance of Casa Salvaje.

A city that has always traded in beautiful things

Cartagena was born to trade. Pedro de Heredia founded it in 1533 on a bay that soon became the gateway to the Americas: gold, silver and emeralds passed through on their way to Seville, and silks, wines and European objects came in. So much wealth drew pirates (Francis Drake sacked the city in 1586), and the answer was the walls that still surround it, declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984.

Shopping in the old city is, in a way, carrying on that story. Today's shops fill houses with wooden balconies and bougainvillea patios that once belonged to merchants, convents and Creole families, on streets that still carry the names of those days.

And it's impossible to walk these streets without thinking of Gabriel García Márquez. He started out here as a journalist, and here, without ever naming it, he set the city of Love in the Time of Cholera. He insisted that in the Caribbean reality outdoes fiction; Cartagena proves it on every corner. So this guide goes from shop to shop, but also from story to story.

St Dom

Plaza Santo Domingo is the heart of the walled city. The church of Santo Domingo, one of Cartagena's oldest, looks over it, and among the café tables lies Fernando Botero's Gertrudis, a naked bronze woman everyone touches for luck.

The concept store that put Cartagena on the design map. It opened in 2014 in a colonial house with a patio and garden, steps from Plaza Santo Domingo, founded by Alex Srour and Maya Memovic, the same founders as the beachwear brand Azulu.

Inside, fashion, jewellery, books, art and homeware by Colombian and Latin American designers sit side by side, from established names like Johanna Ortiz and Amelia Toro to new labels you won't find anywhere else. The New York Times, Forbes and Vogue have all covered it.

What to look for: a dress by a Colombian designer, a handmade piece of jewellery or a design book to take home.

Johanna Ortiz

The most coveted Colombian designer abroad founded her label in Cali in 2003 and is now sold at Bergdorf Goodman, Saks Fifth Avenue and Moda Operandi. She calls Cartagena her second home, and it shows: her ruffles, full sleeves and tropical prints look made for this city.

With Lauren Santo Domingo she published Cartagena Grace with Assouline, a portrait of the city through their eyes. Her boutique is on Plaza Santo Domingo.

What to look for: a long silk or linen dress for dinner nights, and pieces you'll only find in Cartagena.

Silvia Tcherassi

Born on the shores of the Colombian Caribbean, she started as an interior designer and ended up in fashion: her collections have shown on the official calendars of Paris and Milan fashion weeks.

Her boutique in the walled city is small and full of light, with what she does best: light whites, fabrics that breathe and silhouettes designed for Cartagena's heat, made with the same care she'd give a house.

What to look for: a white dress or a linen set for the day, the kind that works from breakfast to dinner.

Lina Cantillo

The shop sits in one of the most legend-laden places in the city. The Sofitel Legend Santa Clara was the convent of the Poor Clares, built in the early seventeenth century. In 1949 a young reporter named García Márquez watched its crypts being emptied, and from one tomb came a copper-coloured mane of hair more than twenty-two metres long. That image became Of Love and Other Demons, the story of Sierva María, the girl raised among slaves and locked away in this very convent.

The Barranquilla designer has been dressing men for more than twenty years and is one of the most recognised voices in Colombian menswear, with shows at Colombiamoda and Bogotá Fashion Week.

Her Cartagena shop is inside the Sofitel Legend Santa Clara hotel, by the pool: printed shirts, guayaberas, linen and resort pieces with real craft, for the man who wants to look sharp without looking like a tourist.

What to look for: a guayabera or a printed shirt for the night at SLVJ.

Soloio

Plaza San Pedro is named after Peter Claver, the Jesuit who spent nearly forty years meeting the enslaved Africans arriving at the port, tending and baptising them. He called himself “the slave of the slaves forever”. His church and cloister are still there, and his body lies beneath the altar.

Spanish menswear founded in 1994, with linen as its signature and 70% of its pieces made in Italy. It arrived in Cartagena in 2016 and now has a shop by Plaza San Pedro.

It's relaxed Mediterranean elegance brought to the Caribbean: shirts, trousers and shorts in natural fabrics that stand up to the heat, plus swimwear in its Soloio Mare line.

What to look for: a light-coloured linen shirt and swim shorts for a day on the islands.

The Relicka

A Cartagena label with a story worth telling: all its clothing is 100% hand-woven by Colombian women who head their households.

It works with everything from chunky knits to soft fabrics and crochet, and its Caribeña collection was born for this climate: fresh, breathable pieces that look nothing like mass production.

What to look for: a crochet piece or a light hand-woven knit, for the beach or for the night.

Website · See on the map

Bahama Mama

Swimwear and beachwear made in Cartagena since 2014, inspired by the Colombian Caribbean. Its designers, Nicole Acosta and Rocío Borré, work with recycled fabrics, cotton, linen and eco-friendly digital printing.

In 2022 it showed at Miami Swim Week alongside other Colombian labels. Everything is designed and made in Colombia.

What to look for: a printed swimsuit and a cover-up for a day in Barú or the Rosario Islands.

Website · See on the map

For the home

Casa Chiqui

Cartagena's most iconic home store, on Calle de la Universidad. It's almost a travel cabinet: furniture and objects its owner has picked on her travels, from Bali to Morocco and South America.

It also has its own line of jewellery, bags and accessories made by Colombian artisans.

What to look for: something for the home that tells a journey, or a piece from its own jewellery line.

Website · See on the map

Atticca

In a city where every house is more than three centuries old, antiques aren't decoration: they're memory. The mahogany furniture, ironwork and church objects that arrived on the galleons or were made here from Caribbean woods still turn up in the houses of the old city, and some of them end up in shops like this one.

Colonial antiques at Calle del Cuartel No. 36-35, in the heart of the old city: furniture, objects and pieces from the same era as the walled city's houses.

It's the place for anyone after something no one else will have, with Cartagena's history included.

What to look for: a colonial piece with a past: furniture or a small object that fits in your suitcase.

Before heading home

As the afternoon fades and the shops close, the city changes its skin. Under the Portal de los Dulces, once the Arcade of the Scribes, Florentino Ariza wrote love letters for hire in García Márquez's novel, and the same coconut sweets and candies are still sold there today. It's the moment to drop the bags and sit down to dinner: night in Cartagena starts late and ends later.

A tip

Shops open by day and most close as the afternoon fades: shop early and end with an aperitivo at La Domenica or dinner at Kanibal or SLVJ. We're at Calle Santa Teresa #32-41; check opening hours and see where to eat in the old city.
